The Clerk's Arithmetic Manual

How to do math on numbers too big to hold in your head —
by picturing machines you can hold in your head.

0The machine: a four-wheel odometer

Forget bits for a moment. Picture the thing in your car that counts miles: a row of wheels, each wheel marked 0 through 9, and when a wheel rolls past 9 it snaps back to 0 and clicks the wheel to its left forward one notch. That's mechanical addition — nobody thinks, the wheels do it.

Our clerk works with the same machine, except each wheel has \(2^{64}\) positions instead of 10 — a wheel with more markings than there are grains of sand on Earth — and there are exactly four wheels.

Each wheel is one uint64_t — one limb. The number itself is the sum of the wheels times their place values:

\[ \text{number} = l_3\,B^3 + l_2\,B^2 + l_1\,B + l_0 \qquad\text{where } B = 2^{64} \]
The one rule of the room: position is place value. The same digit means \(5\), \(5\cdot 2^{64}\), or \(5\cdot 2^{128}\) depending on which wheel holds it — and whatever would have gone on a fifth wheel simply doesn't happen. The odometer rolls back to zero and keeps going. Mathematicians call this arithmetic mod \(2^{256}\); a mechanic calls it rolling the odometer. Every operation below is a consequence of this picture.

0.5The clock face: where the numbers live

Ordinary numbers live on a line that goes on forever. The clerk's numbers live on a clock. There are exactly \(2^{256}\) positions on the dial, and after the last one the hand clicks back to zero.

Walk clockwise to add. Walk counter-clockwise to subtract. Take one step backward from 0 and you land on MAX — the biggest possible number. That's why, on this clock, \(0 - 1 = \text{MAX}\). It's not an error. It's just… one step counter-clockwise.

This is the deepest idea in the manual, so keep it in your pocket: every reading on the dial is two numbers at once. The all-ones pattern is \(2^{256}-1\) if you read it unsigned, and \(-1\) if you read it signed. Same ink, two readings — signed and unsigned aren't different numbers, they're different questions you ask about the same number. The top half of the dial (bit 255 set) is the negative half.

1Comparing: judge the contest from the big end

Which is bigger, 4,700 or 4,699? You never look at the ones digit — the hundreds digits (7 vs 6) already settled it. Same rule with wheels: compare the biggest wheels first, walk right, and the first wheel that disagrees decides everything.

    a:  [0] [0] [5] [9]
    b:  [0] [0] [7] [1]
                 ^
    wheel 3:  0 = 0   tie
    wheel 2:  0 = 0   tie
    wheel 1:  5 < 7   DECIDED -> a < b   (wheel 0 never consulted)

One real algorithm; the rest are free: \(a > b\) means \(b < a\), \(a \le b\) means \(\lnot(b < a)\).

2Adding: wheels rolling over

Adding is ticking the odometer forward, one wheel at a time, right to left, carrying rollovers leftward. And here's the lovely trick for spotting a rollover: when a wheel wraps past its maximum, the total it shows comes out smaller than what you added.

    s = a.l[i] + b.l[i]
    carry = (s < a.l[i])      // wrapped? you owe the next wheel 1

Walkthrough — add 1 to a number whose small wheel is already maxed out (\(M = 2^{64}-1\)):

    a = [0] [0] [0] [M]
    b = [0] [0] [0] [1]

    wheel 0:  M + 1 rolls over to 0 ... and kicks a 1 into wheel 1
    wheel 1:  0 + 0 + 1 (the kick) = 1
    result:   [0] [0] [1] [0]  =  2^64   ✓

And if the last wheel overflows? The kick falls off the edge of the page. Remember the clock — that's just wrapping past the top. \(\text{MAX} + 1\) really is \(0\). The arithmetic is doing \(\bmod\ 2^{256}\) for free.

3Subtracting: winding backwards

Same machine, wound the other way. When a wheel needs to go below 0 it can't — so it borrows a full turn from the wheel to its left: the left wheel winds back one notch, the right wheel wraps to nearly-max and keeps paying.

    U(0, 1, 0, 0) - 1   (that is, 2^64 - 1)

    wheel 0:  0 - 1 can't pay, borrows: reads M, left wheel owes 1
    wheel 1:  1 - 1 (the debt) = 0
    result:   U(M, 0, 0, 0)   ✓
The last borrow is forgiven. \(0 - 1\) rolls the whole odometer backwards one tick and it reads all-max: 0xFFFF…. The machine can't write "negative one"; it can only show the position one tick before zero. Which is exactly \(-1\)'s identity. Negatives live at the top of the dial, counting down from MAX: \(-1 = \text{MAX}\), \(-2 = \text{MAX} - 1\), all the way to \(-2^{255} = \texttt{0x80..00}\).

4Bitwise: the only antisocial ops

&, |, ^, ~ are the lazy operations — the wheels don't talk to each other at all. Bit \(k\) of the answer depends only on bit \(k\) of the two inputs. No clicks, no borrows, no spill.

    r.l[i] = a.l[i] & b.l[i];     // four independent ANDs. done.

Picture two wheels held face to face as stencils: every hole that lines up lets light through (&), any hole in either lets light through (|), holes that differ let light through (^). No wheel ever needs to know what its neighbor did. That's why they're the warm-up: the machinery that makes arithmetic hard — things crossing wheel boundaries — simply isn't present.

5Shifts: the conveyor belt and the doorway

Now picture all 256 bits as beads on a conveyor belt. << n pulls the belt \(n\) places left. Two things happen at once: beads reaching the left edge fall off the page, and beads sliding off the top of one wheel land at the bottom of the wheel above — the wheels aren't welded shut.

Every shift splits as \(n = 64\cdot(n/64) + (n\bmod 64)\): whole wheels slide over, then the leftover bits slide within each wheel. Think of it as two separate moves: first the wheels themselves hop positions, then every bit slides a little more inside its wheel.

How does a destination wheel know who ended up inside it? Ask the question backwards — who was standing toward the small end before the slide? If every wheel hopped left by limb_shift positions, then the wheel now inside wheel \(i\) started limb_shift positions below it: src = i - limb_shift. That's the whole rule — the destination reads downward, and if src is negative, nobody reaches it, so it stays zero. And the doorway donor is always the wheel one position below the source (src - 1): its top bits are exactly the ones the slide pushed off its own top edge.

\[ \underbrace{(\text{src wheel} \ll 10)}_{\text{main slide}} \ \Big|\ \ \underbrace{(\text{wheel below} \gg (64-10))}_{\text{spill through the doorway}} \]

Your own trace said it best: shifting by \(2\cdot 64 + 10\), wheel \(l_3\) gets \(l_1\) slid up and left by 10 — plus the top 10 bits of \(l_0\) (its bits 63..54), fetched by shifting \(l_0\) right by \(64 - 10 = 54\). OR the two pieces and the doorway is rebuilt. >> is the mirror: belt pulls right, beads fall off \(l_0\)'s right edge, zeros feed into \(l_3\)'s top.

The trap: when the leftover is 0 (<< 64, << 128), the spill formula computes >> 64 — and shifting a 64-bit value by 64 is undefined behavior: the hardware quietly masks the count, >> 64 acts like >> 0, and garbage leaks in through the doorway. Guard every spill term with if (bit_shift && ...). It's the classic multi-limb bug: looks right, passes half the tests, corrupts exact-multiple-of-64 shifts.

6Addressing: which wheel, which tick

bit, byte_at, bit_length all ask the same question: given a bit index, which wheel and which position on it?

    wheel    = i / 64        which wheel holds bit i
    position = i % 64        where on that wheel

    bit(a, i)   = (a.l[i/64] >> (i%64)) & 1    // slide to the end, mask
    set bit i:   q.l[i/64] |= 1 << (i%64)       // OR a one-bead mask

One deliberate quirk: byte_at counts from the big end — byte 0 is the most significant byte — because the EVM's BYTE opcode reads the number the way a human reads 4702, left to right. Everything else in the room counts from the small end. Keep the two conventions separate.

7Multiplying: the area picture

Here's the moment it clicks. Don't think "multiply digits" — think area. \(a \times b\) is the area of a rectangle with sides \(a\) and \(b\). Cut each side into its four wheels and the big rectangle tiles into 16 small ones:

Why do tiles on a diagonal share a column? Wheel \(a_i\) is worth \(B^i\), wheel \(b_j\) is worth \(B^j\), so tile \((i,j)\) is a rectangle \(B^i\) wide and \(B^j\) tall: its area is \(a_i b_j \cdot B^{i+j}\). The column index is the exponent — you never compute \(B^{i+j}\), you just drop the tile's value into slot \(i+j\).

One more physical fact: a wheel-times-wheel product doesn't fit on a wheel. \(9 \times 9 = 81\) — a digit times a digit gives a two-digit answer. Ours is worse: 64-bit \(\times\) 64-bit = up to 128 bits = two wheels. The hardware computes both halves at once; C++ hides the top half unless you ask. _umul128 is how you ask:

Walkthrough — \(U(0,1,0,0) \times U(0,1,0,0)\), i.e. \(2^{64} \times 2^{64}\): the only nonzero tile is \((i{=}1, j{=}1)\), so its \(lo\) lands on wheel 2, \(hi\) on wheel 3 (it's 0 anyway). Result: \(U(0,0,1,0) = 2^{128}\). ✓

Carries here are different from addition: the thing flowing upward isn't a flag, it's hi — a whole wheel's worth, up to \(B-2\). Why \(B-2\) max? The biggest tile is \((B-1)^2 = (B-2)B + 1\) — the "9×9=81, you can never carry a 10" argument.

8Dividing: the measuring stick

Division answers a child's question: how many times does the stick \(b\) fit along the rope \(a\)? In decimal long division you guess each digit — how many times does 37 go into 128? Binary removes the guesswork: the quotient digit can only be 0 or 1. Either the stick fits or it doesn't.

The machine: build the answer one bit at a time, most significant first. Keep a "remainder scoop" \(r\). Each round, slide the scoop one place and drop the next bit of \(a\) into it — the "bring down the next digit" step from school. Then ask the only question: does the stick fit in the scoop? Yes → subtract it and write a 1; no → write a 0 and move on.

Walkthrough — \(13 / 3\) (\(a = 1101_2\), \(b = 0011_2\)):

    i=3:  scoop = 0<<1 | 1 = 1    stick fits? no           q = 0000
    i=2:  scoop = 1<<1 | 1 = 3    fits? yes -> scoop = 0   q = 0100
    i=1:  scoop = 0<<1 | 0 = 0    fits? no                 q = 0100
    i=0:  scoop = 0<<1 | 1 = 1    fits? no                 q = 0100

    q = 4, scoop left over = 1     check: 13 = 4*3 + 1   ✓

r = (r << 1) | bit(a, i) is "slide the scoop, drop the next bit in." % is the same machine returning the scoop instead of the quotient.

Rulebook quirks: \(x / 0 = 0\), \(x \bmod 0 = 0\). The clerk doesn't panic — division by zero is a defined answer, not an error.

9Signed ops: same dial, different reading

No new machinery — slt, sgt, sdiv, smod are the clock-face section applied. Bit 255 = the sign flag. Signed compare: if signs differ, the negative one is smaller; same sign, compare as usual — remembering that among negatives, bigger magnitude means more negative. Signed division: unsigned-divide the magnitudes, then paint the sign back on — the quotient takes the XOR of the signs, the remainder follows the dividend's sign.

10addmod / mulmod: the hidden wide desk

Trap ops. \((a + b) \bmod n\) looks like "add, then mod" — but \(a + b\) can roll the odometer, and the rolled-over reading is a different number mod \(n\):

    addmod(MAX, MAX, 3):
        naive:   (MAX + MAX) wraps to 2^256 - 2;  mod 3 = 2   WRONG
        true:    2^257 - 2 mod 3 = 0                          RIGHT

The clerk needs a hidden wider worksheet — a 257-bit scratch area for add, a full 512-bit one for mul — that never goes on the visible desk. mulmod is why the multiplication chapter matters so much: you need the full 512-bit rectangle before you can fold it mod \(n\).

11Conversions: the direction you read

Inside the room: little-endian wheels (wheel 0 is small). On paper, on the wire, in test vectors: big-endian — most significant byte first, like 4702. Every conversion is just walking in the right direction:

    from_bytes(p):  p[0] is the BIG end -> top of wheel 3
                    p[n-1] is small end -> bottom of wheel 0
    to_bytes32:     reverse walk, pad zeros on the LEFT
    from_hex:       digits arrive big-end-first too

•Rules of the room

    - The odometer has four wheels and no fifth: everything wraps mod 2^256.
    - Carries and borrows between wheels are real; off the edge, forgiven.
    - A wheel is a digit in base 2^64. Position IS place value.
    - Wheel x wheel = two wheels. Quotient digits are only 0 or 1.
    - The clerk never panics: x/0 = 0, and a clean stop is a valid answer.
    - Signed and unsigned are two readings of one dial, not two numbers.
